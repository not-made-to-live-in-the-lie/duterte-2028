01 · The deception was premeditated

Sara's "humble van" moment was staged.

The "chance" encounter at a Cebu hotel — a senior pastor watching Sara Duterte step out of a beaten-up old van — was theatre. Long, starstruck posts followed. How humble. How relatable.

Meanwhile: private aircraft between Davao and Manila, several times a week. ₱6.77 billion in large and suspicious transactions through Sara Duterte's accounts (AMLC, 2026, House impeachment hearings). The humility was a costume. The receipts were always the truth.

Pastors deceived. Voters deceived. Whole families deceived. The point is not that you were taken in — the point is that the people who deceived you have not yet been held to account, and you are still being asked to vote for their next bid.

02 · The "we deserve our leaders" line was gaslighting

The verse is real. The deployment was a trap.

Hosea 13:11, 1 Samuel 8, Job 34:30 (Douay-Rheims) — the verses exist. None of them ends the conversation. Scripture's response to "we deserved this" is always repentance and turning, never resignation.

Ask the question the gaslighters do not want you to ask: who benefits when Filipinos accept that they got what they deserved, look down at their shoes, and move on? The people who built the deception. The people who need you to remain inside the trap. Stop quoting the verse as if it closes the case. It does not. It opens it.

03 · Trillanes was right all along

Ten years. Then the receipts dropped.

In 2016, Senator Antonio Trillanes IV filed an affidavit alleging that the Duterte family had received ₱181.6 million in checks from alleged Davao drug lord Samuel "Sammy" Uy between 2011 and 2013. He was mocked for it for ten years.

In April 2026, the AMLC executive director testified before the House Justice Committee: "for all the dates and the amounts mentioned, there are similar transactions existing in the AMLC report." Eighteen of Sara Duterte's transactions match the Trillanes affidavit dates and amounts exactly.

We owed Trillanes more than a decade of mockery. The Filipinos who called him deluded should sit, briefly, with that.

04 · The dead were ours · and most of us looked away

Kian. Carl. Reynaldo. Jee. ~30,000 more.

Kian Loyd delos Santos, 17, Caloocan student. Witness testimony: "Please, that's enough — I have a test tomorrow." Shot anyway. Planted gun, planted shabu, three police convicted of murder.

Reynaldo "Kulot" de Guzman, 14, found with 30 stab wounds and packing tape around his head. Carl Angelo Arnaiz, 19, tortured then shot. Jee Ick-joo, Korean businessman, strangled inside Camp Crame by the very PNP Anti-Illegal Drugs Group "fighting" drugs.

Human Rights Watch estimate: 12,000-30,000 total deaths. The government will admit 6,252 from "police operations." Both numbers are the same indictment. Most of the pulpits in this country said nothing while these names accumulated.

To the family of Kian, to the families of Carl Arnaiz and Reynaldo de Guzman, to every family of a drug-war victim — and to anyone reading this who has lost a loved one to homicide of any kind, in any year, related to the drug war or not: every name above is held with reverence, not as a statistic. This page exists because those lives mattered, and because the silence has lasted too long. The same insistence is owed to every loved one not named here. We will keep saying these names — and we honor every name not yet spoken aloud.
05 · You were never going to be on the list

The class pattern was always the point.

The dead came from informal settlements: Caloocan alleys, Tondo backlots, Payatas slopes. The pulpit congregations that supported — or quietly tolerated — the drug war came from gated subdivisions, condominiums, professional-class suburbs. Two Philippines. One paid for the other's policy.

Amnesty International titled its 2017 report "If you are poor, you are killed." ICC witness testimony: "Oplan Tokhang had to target the poor because they have no capacity to sue."

If 30,000 had been killed in Forbes Park, BGC, or Ayala Heights, the drug war would have been stopped in a week. The fact that this sentence reads as common sense is the whole indictment. The cartoon in your head when the pulpit said "drug addict" was poor for a reason. The killings did not surprise anyone who paid attention to which barangays were being visited.

07 · The mirror

Six questions, undiluted.

When Kian was on the ground begging — "I have a test tomorrow" — why was I not heartbroken?

Did I quietly receive the "rod of discipline" as a license to kill — when Scripture names "hands that shed innocent blood" as something God hates?

I trusted the humble old van. The receipts show ₱6.77 billion. What does that say about the difference between faithfulness in little and unfaithfulness in much?

Who benefits when I keep saying "we deserved him" and refuse to look further? Whose deception does my silence protect?

If a policeman planted a gun on my son and shot him in an alley — would I still call that justice? Or only because it happened to other people's sons?

When the next Kian dies — and there will be a next Kian if we stay silent — will my voice be there? Or will I have already spent it defending the killers?

Now, what will you do?

You read this far. That is not nothing. The verse was never a verdict — it was an invitation to repent, not to remain. We were not made to live in the lie. What is the truth still calling us to see?
