For those who can take it · 2026

The folly,
direct version.

The composite lie that drug addicts are rapists, killers, and thieves who deserve to die — called what it is. The same research, the same data, none of the polite framing.

⚠
This is the direct version. The main read avoids calling things a lie or a folly at the door — this one does. If sharper words feel uncalled-for, the main version is one click away. Survivors of sexual violence and grieving families: the sharpness on this page is aimed at the policy and the rhetoric, never at you. Inline notes for readers carrying related grief appear at each sub-claim; full support resources are at the bottom of this page.
01 · The composite lie · named

The rhetoric that opened the door to ~30,000 killings.

The fusion was seamless and it was a lie: drug addict → criminal → rapist → killer → thief → deserves death. Each link in that chain is empirically wrong. The chain still worked because it bypassed evidence and went straight for the gut. And the unstated qualifier was always: the poor addict will rape your daughter, never the rich one. The wealthy addict goes to private rehab. The poor addict gets a bullet in an alley.

"Hitler massacred 3 million Jews. Now there is 3 million drug addicts… I'd be happy to slaughter them." — Rodrigo Duterte, Davao, Sep 30 2016 · Inquirer / CNN / Bloomberg / AJ / PBS

The man said it on the record. Filipinos clapped. Then 30,000 mostly-poor Filipinos died, and many of us went on with our Sundays.

02 · The three sub-claims · refuted in both directions

The research has been in for decades.

Folly #1 · drug user → rapist
Most drug users do not commit rape. Most rapists are not drug-dependent.
Rape is driven by power, anger, and control, not sexual desire (Groth typology, OJP/DOJ-cited). Most rapes are planned. ~70 % of perpetrators are someone the victim knows (RAINN). The personality and attitudes of perpetrators who drink during assault are the same as those who don't drink (Abbey et al., PMC peer-reviewed review). Drugs disinhibit; they do not create a rapist disposition. The science was never on the side of the killings.
To anyone reading this who carries the weight of sexual assault — whenever it happened, whoever did it, and whether or not any substance was involved: the experience of survivors is real, and what is done in these crimes is never the survivor's fault. The research above describes patterns across the people who commit these crimes — it does not reduce any individual story to a number. The statistics exist here for one reason only: to show that the stylized image used to justify EJKs does not match the evidence — and that the killings did not make survivors any safer. Real safety, real justice, and real accountability remain unfinished work — owed to every survivor, in every case still without closure.
Folly #2 · drug user → killer
Most drug users are non-violent. Most homicide perpetrators are not drug-dependent.
Per BJS / FBI UCR (2007): only 3.9 % of US homicides with known circumstances were narcotics-related. Per BJS 2004 Survey of Inmates: violent offenders were only 10 % likely to have committed the crime for drug money (vs 30 % property, 26 % drug). Most "drug-related" violence is systemic (drug-market turf disputes, per Goldstein 1985, OJP) — not the cartoon addict who snapped. The cartoon was always wrong.
To anyone grieving someone lost to homicide — drug-related, vigilante, EJK, domestic, accident-disguised-as-violence, or any cause at all, whether years ago or recent: this page does not minimize losses like these. Statistics are how we expose policy failures; they are not how anyone measures grief. No loved one in any of these cases is a number. The numbers exist here to show that the policy did not honor them either — it killed in their name without protecting anyone, and the justice these cases deserve has still not been delivered.
Folly #3 · drug user → thief
Most drug users do not steal. Most thieves are not drug-addicted.
The link is real but conditional — concentrated in severe heroin/crack dependence under poverty (Goldstein "economic-compulsive"). 85 % of burglary inmates report substance dependence; only ~25 % committed the crime for drug money. OJP "Professional Thieves and Drugs": criminal activity began before drug use, addiction was incidental. The implied solution is treatment and economic safety nets, not bullets. Never bullets.
To anyone, anywhere, who has been hurt by theft, addiction-adjacent crime, or watched a loved one's substance struggle — whether during the drug war or long before it: that fear was, and is, real for the communities who lived it. The case here is not that those harms were imaginary — it is that bullets in alleys did not protect those communities, and the kingpins were never jailed. The data shows it. Communities that lived with this deserved real safety: rehab access, livelihood, community policing, due process for the few who do harm, and accountability all the way up the chain. Not a war that killed neighbors and left the actual traffickers untouched.
03 · The folly of believing the cartoon

The image carried in our heads was already half the killing.

When the pulpit said "drug addict," what did you picture? Male. Poor. Tattooed. Unpredictable. Dangerous. Already half a criminal. The cartoon was always class-coded. It was never the businessman with a coke habit or the mayor's son with a pill problem. By design, it lived in the slum.

That image — not the actual person killed in an alley — is what most Filipino Christians were defending when they defended the drug war. The folly was not the killing alone. The folly was the picture in your head.

04 · The class war disguised as a drug war

It was never a war on drugs. It was a war on people who couldn't fight back.

Amnesty International's 2017 report title was the policy summary:

"If you are poor, you are killed." — Amnesty International, ASA 35/5517/2017

ICC witness testimony: "Oplan Tokhang had to target the poor because they have no capacity to sue." Deaths concentrated in Caloocan alleys, Tondo backlots, Payatas slopes. Almost none in gated subdivisions.

Meanwhile the alleged drug lord Samuel "Sammy" Uy paid the Duterte family ₱181.6 million (AMLC, House Justice Committee, Apr 2026). Protected. Never raided. Never on a tokhang list. Wealthy addicts went to private rehab. Politicians' sons caught with drugs faced lawyers and plea deals. Tens of thousands of deaths produced almost zero kingpin convictions. The shortcuts were the policy.

05 · The folly of Christian distance

You were never going to be on the list. And that is why the killings did not break your sleep.

The Christians who supported — or quietly tolerated — the drug war were overwhelmingly not the people being killed. The pulpit congregations came from gated subdivisions, condominiums, professional-class suburbs. The dead came from informal settlements. Two Philippines. One paid for the other's policy.

It was easy to support harsher judgment for the poor because the harsh judgment was happening to other people. The pulpit said "drug addict" and the comfortable Christian heard "those people in the slum" — not "my cousin, my uncle, my neighbor."

If 30,000 had been killed in Forbes Park, BGC, or Ayala Heights, the drug war would have been stopped in a week.

"If you really keep the royal law — 'Love your neighbor as yourself' — you are doing right. But if you show favoritism, you sin… You have dishonored the poor." — James 2:8–9, 6 (NIV)

Christian elitism does not look like cruelty. It looks like distance. Like quiet relief that the trouble is in someone else's barangay. That distance was the political condition for the killings.

06 · The mirror

Six questions, undiluted.

When the pulpit said "drug addict," did I picture the shabu-crazed predator from the slum — or a real human being I have ever actually known?

Did I assume a tokhang list was a clinical diagnosis — rather than barangay hearsay collected in a drop box?

Did I conflate "addict" with "rapist," "killer," or "thief" because a politician's rhetoric did — not because the evidence does?

Did I notice that none of the dead came from gated subdivisions? Did my silence cost me nothing because the killings were in someone else's barangay? Was that not the very favoritism James warned against?

Would I want my own struggling cousin or uncle on such a list — with no diagnosis, no lawyer, no right of appeal?

When the next "drug suspect" is shot, will my voice be there — or will I have already spent it defending the killers?

07 · The resolution

We can share the lament. We cannot share the impunity.

The family abused by addiction is innocent. The child harmed by a strung-out neighbor is innocent. Their grief is real. Their cry for safety is righteous. Solving the drug problem and refusing extrajudicial killings are not opposites. Both can be true. Both must be.

But forbearance has limits. You cannot extend "forbearance" to theft and killing dressed up as policy. That is not reconciliation — that is collaboration. The Bible asks forbearance between equals in disagreement; it never asks it of the powerful crushing the powerless.

Impunity is the actual enemy here. The power to kill, steal, and walk away unaccountable. That is what we are fighting — not addicts in slums.

If you got this far, you have the stomach for what comes next.

Re-learn. Re-look. Refuse. Repair. The truth is still calling.
