Filipino Reckonings · 2026 · A Civic Catalog · For the 2028 Ballot

Hindi Na Muli
— The Thousand Cuts.

A Filipino civic catalog of what the institutions of the Republic suffered between 2016 and 2022, and the 2028 deadline to stop the next iteration. Six years. Twenty-five institutions. A pattern not of one collapse but of accumulating injury. The Carnegie Endowment, in its 2019 mid-term assessment of the Duterte presidency, called it "democratic backsliding by a thousand cuts." This deck is the catalog of those cuts — what was broken, what was captured, what was hollowed — assembled in one place so the next Filipino voter standing in a 2028 polling booth has the receipts.

02 · Methodology · What we cite · What we do not claim · What we return to

Twenty-five institutions. One catalog. One thesis.

The data, plainly.

  • Every claim is sourced to first-tier reporting (Rappler, Inquirer, Philstar, GMA, BusinessWorld, MindaNews, Bloomberg, Reuters, CNN, BBC, NYT, Washington Post, Al Jazeera, Time), authoritative academic sources (Carnegie Endowment, ICG, Brookings, Lowy Institute), official documents (SC rulings, COA reports, AMLC findings, UN HRC resolutions), and the records of named civil-society monitors (HRW, Amnesty, Karapatan, CMFR, NUJP, FLAG, IBP, IPRI).
  • Every event is dated to the day where the public record permits; attributed to ≥1 first-tier outlet; linked to a live source on the References slide.
  • Every institution slide answers the question: why this institution specifically? What would it have done if it had remained intact? The gold-bordered "Why this institution" beat is the deck's interpretive contract with the reader.
The libel guard, declared explicitly. This brief does not impute motive to individuals not party to the relevant criminal or administrative proceedings. It does not declare guilt where courts have not ruled. It documents events, dates, and visible consequences. Where a finding is alleged but not yet judicially determined, the brief says so. The reader weighs.

Two standing descriptive disciplines. (1) This brief uses "killed in police operations" as the descriptive baseline; "extrajudicial killing" is used only where so-characterized by the UN Human Rights Council, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, the Philippine Commission on Human Rights, Karapatan, or named investigative reporting. (2) Witness recantations are described as recorded. The brief does not editorialize on which testimony is true; it presents what is on the public record.

Three named registers the brief uses — declared so the reader can tell which mode each passage is in.

  • Damage — the institution was reduced in capacity or independence by direct administrative action: budget cuts, leadership changes, statutory restrictions, dissolution attempts.
  • Capture — the institution remained nominally intact but was staffed, directed, or procedurally constrained by Duterte-aligned principals to the extent that its independent functioning was compromised.
  • Normalization — patterns of public discourse, official conduct, or impunity that lowered the floor of what is politically and culturally acceptable in the Philippines.
The Carnegie anchor. The deck's primary academic frame is "democratic backsliding" as documented by David G. Timberman of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace in his January 10, 2019 working paper Philippine Politics Under Duterte: A Midterm Assessment.[89] Timberman invokes Levitsky & Ziblatt's "death by a thousand cuts" methodology — many government efforts to subvert democracy are legal, operating within constitutional bounds while degrading democratic norms incrementally. He warns specifically: "Most Filipinos under 45 did not experience Marcos abuses, making them less protective of democratic freedoms." That cohort is this deck's actual audience.
Attribution on the subtitle phrase — "The Thousand Cuts." The deck's subtitle is not original to this brief. It draws on, and explicitly credits, two prior usages:
  • Carnegie / Timberman 2019. The framing of Levitsky & Ziblatt's "death by a thousand cuts" methodology applied to the Duterte presidency in the working paper cited above.[89]
  • A Thousand Cuts — the 2020 documentary film by Filipino-American director Ramona S. Diaz about Maria Ressa, Rappler, and the press-freedom struggle under Duterte. Sundance world premiere January 25, 2020; theatrical and virtual release August 7, 2020 (PBS Distribution); Frontline broadcast January 8, 2021.[90] The Diaz documentary is treated in detail on Slide 27 (Rappler / Maria Ressa).
This brief uses the phrase in the same lineage. It does not claim originality on it. Credit to Ramona S. Diaz, to David G. Timberman, and to Levitsky & Ziblatt for the framework all three works draw upon.

03 · The three registers · How to read this brief

Damage. Capture. Normalization.

Every institution in the catalog falls into one or more of three registers. The register tells the reader what kind of remedy is needed — and what kind of 2028 candidate would or would not deliver it.

DAMAGE. The institution was reduced in capacity or independence by direct administrative action. Examples covered in this catalog: CHR (₱1,000 budget threat, September 2017); ABS-CBN (franchise non-renewal, July 2020); Salugpongan / Lumad schools (DepEd permit revocation, October 2019); the LGU project-acceptance rule (quietly removed, then restored only after Marcos). The remedy: legislative restoration, budgetary commitment, and statutory entrenchment that prevents the future executive from repeating the cut.
CAPTURE. The institution remained nominally intact but was staffed, directed, or procedurally constrained by Duterte-aligned principals to the extent that its independent functioning was compromised. Examples covered: Supreme Court (Sereno ouster + ~12-of-15 Duterte appointees); Office of the Ombudsman (Martires SALN-confidentiality memo); DOJ + Office of the Solicitor General (Calida's role across multiple controversies); AFP/PNP + NTF-ELCAC; PS-DBM (Pharmally procurement). The remedy: depoliticization of appointments, statutory term-limits on key offices, and procedural reforms that make capture harder.
NORMALIZATION. Patterns of public discourse, official conduct, or impunity that lowered the floor of what is politically and culturally acceptable in the Philippines. Examples covered: rape jokes and the February 7, 2018 "shoot in the vagina" speech; red-tagging as everyday political rhetoric; killings without prosecution; ICC withdrawal as foreign-policy default. The hardest register to undo — not because the policies cannot be reversed but because the lowered floor of public expectation persists across administrations.

The deck is organized institution-by-institution rather than register-by-register, because most institutions suffered all three registers simultaneously. The register chip on each card on the map of damage (next slide) gives the dominant pattern; the institution slide that follows names the specific damage in detail.

04 · The Carnegie thesis · Death by a thousand cuts

The first Filipino president to not render even the minimum obeisance to liberal democratic politics.

"Because there is no single moment in which the regime crosses the line, nothing may set off alarm bells."
— David G. Timberman, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, January 10, 2019[89]

Four Carnegie attribution lines this deck operationalizes.

The norms argument. "Without robust norms, constitutional checks and balances do not serve as the bulwarks of democracy we imagine them to be." The catalog of damage shows what happens when norms collapse but the constitutional text remains the same.[89]
The police argument. "Inducing police to engage in shoot-to-kill policies is enormously corrosive of law enforcement and the rule of law." Slide 9 (PNP/Tokhang/Kian) is the institutional consequence.[89]
The generational argument. "Most Filipinos under 45 did not experience Marcos abuses, making them less protective of democratic freedoms." This is the deck's actual audience. The catalog is the institutional memory the Carnegie warning predicted would be needed.[89]
The vulnerability argument. Carnegie identifies weak political parties, presidentialism, and malleable institutions as creating "wide latitude" for norm-breaking by an extremist demagogue. The Filipino vote in 2028 is the constitutional check the parties cannot deliver on their own.[89]

This deck operationalizes the Carnegie warning. Each subsequent slide names one institution that was cut. Each cut is a category of damage the next president — whoever wins in 2028 — must either heal or compound.

Part I · The Judiciary · 06 · Capture · The Supreme Court

The chief justice was removed by the body she led.

  • May 11, 2018. The Supreme Court voted 8-6 to grant a quo warranto petition filed by Solicitor General Jose Calida against Chief Justice Maria Lourdes Sereno, nullifying her appointment.[91] Cited reason: her purported failure to file required SALNs before her 2010 appointment as Associate Justice.
  • Unprecedented mechanism. Sereno was the first Philippine Chief Justice removed by quo warranto rather than impeachment. The International Commission of Jurists characterized the ruling as a contribution to "the deterioration of the rule of law."[92]
  • The 8-justice majority that voted to oust: Leonardo-de Castro, Peralta, Bersamin, Jardeleza, Martires, Reyes Jr., Gesmundo, Tijam. The 6-dissent minority: Carpio, Velasco, Del Castillo, Perlas-Bernabe, Leonen, Caguioa.[91]
  • Connect the dots. Samuel Martires voted to oust Sereno here; later that same year he was appointed Ombudsman, where in 2020 he issued the SALN-confidentiality memorandum (Slide 8) restricting public access to the very documents whose alleged non-filing was used to remove Sereno.
  • Political context. Sereno had publicly questioned the drug war.[92]
  • The appointee stack. By the end of his term, Duterte had appointed a substantial majority of sitting Supreme Court justices (commonly cited as ~12 of 15; exact count varies with retirement-cycle).
An intact Supreme Court is the constitutional check that decides which executive actions are lawful: drug-war prosecutions, ICC cooperation, Anti-Terrorism Act designations (Slide 11), election rulings, presidential-power scope, amnesty revocations (Trillanes, Slide 30), franchise non-renewals (ABS-CBN, Slide 26). Every other institutional capture in this catalog has the Supreme Court as its potential corrective. The ruling on Sereno's removal — and the appointee stack that followed — meant the Court itself became less likely to apply that corrective. Carnegie named this directly: "the Supreme Court's autonomy and integrity seriously compromised."[89] A captured Supreme Court is the multiplier that lets every other capture stand.
What the next president inherits: a Supreme Court whose composition was substantially shaped by one administration, sitting on multiple Anti-Terrorism Act challenges, ICC-jurisdiction questions, and drug-war prosecutions still working their way to the Bench. The 2028 candidate due-diligence question: who would they appoint to the next SC vacancies, and on what record?
Part I · The Judiciary · 07 · Capture/Damage · The lower courts

The chair of one court can change what the whole bench sees.

  • Caseload collapse. The Anti-Terrorism Act 2020 (next slide) materially expanded prosecutorial discretion; lower courts faced an unprecedented caseload. Carnegie's 2019 estimate: 289,000+ drug cases filed; prisons at 582% overcapacity; extrajudicial-killing convictions rare.[89]
  • Forum-shopping illustrated: the Trillanes case. Makati RTC Branch 148 (Judge Soriano) denied DOJ's bid to revive coup d'etat charges; Branch 150 (Judge Alameda) reinstated the rebellion case. Two judges, one law, different outcomes.[93]
  • The CA correction. The Court of Appeals later ruled that Branch 150 committed "grave abuse of discretion." But the precedent — that the executive can shop for outcomes among lower-court branches — remained on the public record.[93]
  • The witness-pressure pattern (Slide 29 in detail). The De Lima case relied on testimony from convicts later recanted under oath. Lower-court reliance on coordinated convict testimony is a documented procedural weakness.
Intact lower courts are the daily site of due process. They issue search warrants (the Tumandok warrants on Slide 23 were issued by lower courts and later declared invalid), accept or reject witness testimony (the De Lima testimony was admitted before being recanted), and rule on motions that determine whether the Anti-Terrorism Act framework can be used against a journalist (the Cumpio case, Slide 28). When the procedural floor at the trial-court level lowers, every other higher-court check has more work to do. The pattern of the period: more cases filed, fewer convictions for state-perpetrated killings, more witnesses produced by the prosecution who later recanted under oath.
What the next president inherits: a trial-court system that processed extraordinary volumes of drug-war and red-tagging cases with prosecutorial pressure documented at every level. Restoration requires judicial-training reform, procedural protections for protected witnesses, and statutory limits on forum-shopping by the executive.
Part I · The Judiciary · 08 · Capture · The Office of the Ombudsman

The accountability body stopped showing what the powerful own.

  • 2018: Ombudsman Conchita Carpio-Morales ended her 7-year term. Duterte appointed Samuel Martires — the same justice who had voted to oust Sereno earlier that year (Slide 6).
  • September 1, 2020: Martires signed Memorandum Circular No. 1, series of 2020, restricting public access to SALNs filed at the Ombudsman.[94] SALNs henceforth released only with a notarized letter of authority from the SALN owner; by court order; or via the Ombudsman's own Field Investigation Office.
  • PCIJ test case (July 2021): the Philippine Center for Investigative Journalism requested Duterte's SALN. The request hit a "dead end."[95]
  • Martires' defense. SALNs, he argued, had been "weaponized to damage the reputation of government officials or political rivals." During a September 2021 Senate budget deliberation — a year after the memo — he stated, in defense of the restriction: "No matter how much criticism I will receive, even if I will be bashed, even if I will be removed from office, I will not butt in to that."[94]
  • De Lima request denied. The Ombudsman rejected then-Senator De Lima's bid to make Duterte's SALN public.[96]
  • 2025 restoration: Ombudsman Jesus Crispin "Boying" Remulla announced he would undo the Martires restrictions.[97]
An intact Ombudsman is the body that prosecutes corruption against the most senior officials — and the body whose access to SALN records is the precondition for documenting unexplained wealth. The September 2020 SALN-confidentiality memo, by Martires's own subsequent acknowledgment, was issued to stop the use of SALNs as instruments of public accountability. The institutional consequence: the same public records that would let any voter, journalist, or rival politician detect billionaire-scale anomalies in declared wealth — see the AMLC/SALN mismatch documented in the sibling deck the-fog.html[24] (six SALNs declaring zero cash against ₱230.87M in transactions) — were placed behind a notarized-letter-of-authority wall. Any party with structural interest in keeping unexplained wealth out of public discussion benefits from an Ombudsman that restricts SALN access. The memo did not require legislation. It required only one signature.
What the next president inherits: an Ombudsman whose 2020-2025 memorandum durably narrowed Filipino civic visibility into the wealth of public officials. The Carnegie "norms" argument applies directly — the law did not change; the practice did. Statutory entrenchment of SALN public access would prevent a future Ombudsman from re-imposing the restriction.
Part II · The Coercive Apparatus · 09 · Normalization + Damage · The PNP

A 17-year-old. A basketball court. A CCTV that contradicted the official story.

  • Oplan Tokhang — house-to-house anti-drug operations launched July 2016. Killings during police operations under Duterte: PNP official figure ~6,000; HRW/Amnesty/independent estimates ~30,000.[46]
  • August 16, 2017: Kian delos Santos — 17 years old, killed in Caloocan during a Tokhang operation. CCTV showed plainclothes officers dragging him across a basketball court. Officers claimed self-defense; witnesses contradicted.[98]
  • November 29, 2018: RTC convicted three officers (Arnel Oares, Jeremias Pereda, Jerwin Cruz) of murder; sentenced to reclusion perpetua.[98] August 11, 2025: Supreme Court affirmed the conviction (decision publicly announced December 22, 2025).[98]
  • The "ninja cops" scandal. Police officers who recycled seized drugs back into the trade. November 29, 2013, Mexico, Pampanga: A raid seized 36.68 kg of shabu officially; a Senate investigation later found ₱648 million worth of seized shabu undeclared. Acting Pampanga police chief at the time: Oscar Albayalde — later Duterte's PNP Chief (2018-2019).[99]
  • PDEA chief Aaron Aquino testified that Albayalde had intervened against the dismissal of the 13 "ninja cops."[99] Albayalde resigned amid the scandal in October 2019. Internal cleansing program failed.
  • March 2025 DOJ admission: Justice Secretary (now Ombudsman) Remulla said the local justice system was "weak to prosecute former president Rodrigo Duterte" because prosecutors were under pressure. 95% of wrongful deaths under Tokhang had no police reports.[100]
Kian was 17. His name is the one we say first because no list of these dead can be allowed to flatten into a number. He had an exam the next day. CCTV showed officers dragging him to a dark area near a river before he was shot. The Supreme Court has now affirmed twice that the official story was false. He is one of perhaps thirty thousand.
An intact PNP is the daily civilian instrument of public safety — and the institutional check that, when functioning, prevents the executive from operationalizing a death-squad doctrine in uniform. The Carnegie report named this in 2019: "Inducing police to engage in shoot-to-kill policies is enormously corrosive of law enforcement and the rule of law."[89] When the highest officer in the institution (the PNP Chief Albayalde) himself was alleged to have intervened to protect drug-recycling subordinates, the institution's internal accountability mechanisms could not have caught Tokhang abuses even if the political will had existed at lower ranks. The damage is not just to the 30,000 dead; it is to the institution's capacity to ever again be trusted as an honest enforcement body without comprehensive structural reform.
What the next president inherits: a police institution whose internal accountability mechanisms demonstrably failed at every level from operations to discipline to prosecution. The 2028 candidate due-diligence question: what is the candidate's documented record on PNP reform, internal-affairs strengthening, and prosecution of Tokhang-era abuses?
Part II · The Coercive Apparatus · 10 · Capture + Normalization · The military and red-tagging

Red-tagging was a daily official practice with a body count.

  • December 4, 2018: Executive Order 70 established the National Task Force to End Local Communist Armed Conflict (NTF-ELCAC), with Cabinet-level representation. The "Whole-of-Nation" approach broadened the red-tagging architecture beyond the AFP.
  • The named officials: Lt. Gen. Antonio Parlade Jr. (NTF-ELCAC spokesperson) and Lorraine Badoy-Partosa (undersecretary) became the most-cited public faces of administration red-tagging.
  • September 2023: the Office of the Ombudsman convicted both Parlade and Badoy of "conduct prejudicial to the best interest of the service" for red-tagging activists.[101]
  • The body count. Karapatan has documented 427 activists killed after being red-tagged.[101]
  • Named killings include peace consultant Randall Echanis (killed in his Quezon City home on August 10, 2020) and Karapatan legal worker Zara Alvarez (shot dead in Bacolod City on August 17, 2020 — the 13th Karapatan human-rights worker killed under the Duterte administration per Karapatan's documentation).[101]
  • UN Special Rapporteur: red-tagging vilification is "often followed by threats, unlawful surveillance, attacks or even unlawful killing."[101]
  • May 2024 Supreme Court ruling: red-tagging threatens "a person's right to life, liberty, or security."[101] HRW and Karapatan called for NTF-ELCAC's abolition.
Randall and Zara were named civic workers doing legal, observable work — consultancies and legal aid for families of the dispossessed. They were killed after the administration's red-tagging apparatus named them publicly. They are two of four hundred and twenty-seven Karapatan documents.
An intact AFP confines itself to defense from external and armed-internal threats; an intact civilian information space confines public discourse to evidence-based contestation. The NTF-ELCAC architecture — specifically the daily public broadcasting of red-tagging accusations against named individuals and organizations — created a documented operational pipeline from public accusation to extrajudicial violence. The Supreme Court itself acknowledged the causal link in 2024. Any party with structural interest in suppressing civil-society organizing benefits when red-tagging is a routine official practice with no immediate consequence for the official making the accusation. The 2023 Ombudsman convictions of Parlade and Badoy were the first procedural correction; the architecture itself remains.
What the next president inherits: a red-tagging institutional architecture the Supreme Court itself has ruled threatens the right to life — and which has not yet been dismantled. The 2028 candidate due-diligence question (Watchlist #3): has the candidate publicly committed to abolishing NTF-ELCAC?
Part II · The Coercive Apparatus · 11 · Damage · The Anti-Terrorism Act 2020

A law that lets the executive name terrorists. Mostly upheld. The chill is the point.

  • July 3, 2020: Republic Act 11479 (Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020) signed by Duterte amid the early pandemic, repealing the 2007 Human Security Act.
  • The Anti-Terrorism Council (ATC). Empowered to designate persons or organizations as terrorists administratively — no court hearing — based on its own findings or on requests from foreign states or international bodies (the latter struck down in 2021; the former preserved).[102]
  • December 9, 2021: Supreme Court ruled on 37 consolidated challenges. Only two provisions struck down: (a) the Section 4 qualifier that could have construed advocacy/protest/work stoppage as terrorism; and (b) Section 25 paragraph 2 (the foreign-request designation pathway). The bulk of the ATA stands.[103]
  • The chilling effect. The Cumpio case (Slide 28) — journalist arrested February 2020, charged under ATA's terrorism-financing provision in October 2021, convicted in 2026 — is the prototype of how ATA designations function in practice against named civilian critics.[104]
  • January 15, 2024: SC issued procedural rules for judicial relief from ATC designation and freeze orders, partially formalizing the appeal pathway.[103]
A democratic state's anti-terrorism architecture is by design subject to extraordinary procedural safeguards because the state's pre-trial powers under it (surveillance, asset freezes, detention without warrant in some jurisdictions) are extraordinary. The ATA shifted the locus of designation from a judicial process to an executive council, with limited pre-designation due process. Once designation is administrative, the procedural cost of using designation as a political tool against named critics drops — even if the eventual conviction rate is low, the pretrial chilling effect runs ahead of the eventual ruling. Frenchie Mae Cumpio's six years of pretrial detention is the institutional consequence: the conviction came in 2026, but the silence came in 2020.
What the next president inherits: an ATA designation pathway whose structural defaults favor executive discretion. The 2028 candidate due-diligence question (Watchlist #6): what reforms to ATC designation procedure has the candidate publicly committed to?
Part II · The Coercive Apparatus · 12 · Capture · Customs, Corrections, and the drug-trade infrastructure

The same official. Two scandals. Two billion-peso costs.

  • Bureau of Customs, May 26, 2017: Commissioner Nicanor Faeldon at the helm during the ₱6.4-billion shabu shipment from China — 602.2 kg seized in a Valenzuela warehouse raid.[105]
  • Faeldon resigned amid the scandal; DOJ initially dismissed charges; the Ombudsman later recommended filing criminal and administrative charges against Faeldon and other senior BOC officials.[105]
  • Bureau of Corrections, 2019: Faeldon (now BuCor Director General) at the center of the GCTA scandal — the Good Conduct Time Allowance law was being used to release convicted criminals early. The near-release of convicted rapist-murderer Antonio Sanchez (former Calauan mayor convicted 1995 of multiple rape-with-homicide) triggered national outcry. Faeldon was relieved.
  • The pattern. The same senior official survived a billion-peso drug-shipment scandal in one agency only to preside over a near-release-of-convicted-rapist-murderer scandal in another. Two crises that should have ended a career; neither did, in time.
An intact Bureau of Customs is the first physical interdiction layer against drug imports; an intact Bureau of Corrections is the last institutional layer of accountability for convicted violent offenders. Both bureaus, under the same senior appointee, demonstrated billion-peso failure of their core mandates within a 24-month window. The structural incentive for keeping the same official across both failures — a personal-loyalty staffing pattern documented across the period — is not consistent with merit-based bureau governance. Carnegie identified this pattern as one of the period's defining features: institutions staffed by loyalty rather than competence are easier for the executive to deploy and harder for the public to hold accountable.

Part II · The Coercive Apparatus · 13 · Normalization · The ICC exit

Withdrawing from the world's accountability court did not erase its jurisdiction.

  • March 17, 2018: Duterte deposited notice of withdrawal from the Rome Statute, ~1 month after the ICC chief prosecutor's preliminary examination of the drug war was announced.[106]
  • March 17, 2019: Withdrawal took effect.[106]
  • The jurisdictional point. Under Article 127 of the Rome Statute, the ICC retains jurisdiction over crimes committed while the country was still a member — November 1, 2011 through March 16, 2019.[106]
  • September 15, 2021: ICC Pre-Trial Chamber I authorized the Prosecutor to open a full investigation of the Philippine drug war for crimes committed during the membership window.[107]

The International Criminal Court is the international community's standing answer to the post-1945 question: where is the venue when domestic institutions cannot or will not prosecute state-perpetrated mass killings? The withdrawal removed the Philippines from the Rome Statute's go-forward jurisdiction but did not erase the jurisdiction that existed during the membership window — precisely because that retention is built into the treaty by design. The political signal — we do not recognize this court — was the operational point, more than the jurisdictional escape.

Part V · Local-Governance Sabotage · 22 · Damage · The LGU sign-off rule that vanished

The quality-control safeguard against ghost projects — quietly removed, then restored only after the next administration.

  • The rule. Longstanding administrative procedure requiring local government units (LGUs) to formally accept (sign off on) completed national-government infrastructure projects before turnover — a quality-assurance step that prevents "ghost projects" (projects officially completed on paper but defective or never built in fact).
  • What happened during the period. Per President Ferdinand Marcos Jr.'s subsequent statement: "The rule, which President Marcos said was a safeguard to ensure the quality of nationally funded infrastructure projects, was removed by the previous administration."[117]
  • The restoration. 2024-2025: Marcos ordered all government agencies to reinstate the LGU-acceptance process for completed national projects.[117] The League of Cities of the Philippines publicly backed the directive.[118]
  • Why this matters. Removing LGU sign-off means the same executive that builds the project also signs off that the project meets standards. The acceptance rule existed precisely to introduce an independent local check on national-government project quality. Without it: ghost projects pass through the chain.
An intact LGU project-acceptance procedure is the most basic federalism-grounded quality check on national infrastructure spending: the city or municipality that will live with the road, the bridge, the flood-control system is the body best positioned to confirm it works. The removal of this rule — an administrative change requiring no legislation, attracting limited press coverage at the time — converted the project-acceptance step from an independent check to an executive self-certification. This is the textbook example of Carnegie's "death by a thousand cuts" mechanism: the law did not change; the public was not consulted; the procedural safeguard simply vanished, and only became visible when the next administration had to explicitly restore it. The restoration is the receipt that the removal happened.
What the next president inherits: an LGU-acceptance procedure that has been formally restored but whose absence for years means an unknowable number of national projects were turned over without LGU quality validation. The 2028 audit question (Watchlist #11): will the candidate commit to keeping the rule restored AND to a public audit of 2017-2022 national projects that were turned over without LGU sign-off?

The Cumpio case demonstrates how the Anti-Terrorism Act framework (Slide 11) functions in practice against named civilian critics: a charge under ATA's terrorism-financing provision can be added to existing charges to extend detention; the case's slow movement through trial-court processes becomes itself the chilling instrument. The eventual conviction is the legal endpoint; the six pretrial years are the operational point. Any party with structural interest in suppressing critical coverage of security forces benefits when the procedural cost of detention runs ahead of the legal cost of conviction.
What the next president inherits: the ATA + pretrial-detention combination as a documented architecture for silencing named journalists for years before any verdict. The 2028 due-diligence question: what reforms to pretrial-detention duration, ATA terrorism-financing standards, and journalist-protection protocols does the candidate publicly commit to?
Part VIII · Political Prisoners · 29 · Damage · Sen. Leila de Lima

A sitting senator detained for nearly seven years on testimony five key witnesses later recanted under oath.

  • February 23-24, 2017: arrest warrant issued; De Lima surrendered.[134] She had led Senate investigations of the Davao Death Squad (Slide 35) and the early drug war.
  • The recantations. Five key prosecution witnesses later recanted their testimonies; sources also note that by 2023 a broader set of at least 13 witnesses had recanted across the various cases:
    • ·Kerwin Espinosa: said he had been "coerced, pressured, intimidated, and seriously threatened" to make his prior allegations.[134]
    • ·Rafael Ragos: former BuCor head; retracted his testimony that he had delivered money from drug lords to De Lima; said he was "forced by government officials to make it."[134]
    • ·Ronnie Dayan: former bodyguard and co-accused; recanted earlier testimony.[134]
    • ·Rodolfo Magleo: informed De Lima he was recanting; had previously claimed Bilibid drug lords were paying commissions.[134]
    • ·Nonilo Arile: similarly recanted; had accused De Lima of drug-trade involvement.[134]
  • February 2021: first acquittal.
  • May 2023: second acquittal. Trial Court Judge Abraham Alcantara: "Without [the recanted] testimony, the crucial link to establish conspiracy is shrouded with reasonable doubt."[135]
  • November 13, 2023: final release after final acquittal.[134]
  • Total detention: ~6 years, 9 months.
  • Methodology note: the brief presents the witness recantations as a matter of public record. It does not editorialize on which testimony — the original or the recantation — was truthful. The brief observes only that the prosecution lost the conspiracy link when the recantations were submitted under oath, and the court ruled accordingly.
Sen. De Lima had led the Senate investigation of the Davao Death Squad (Slide 35) in the months before her arrest. She had publicly criticized the drug war from her Senate seat. The detention was the consequence of the criticism; the recantations are the legal receipt that the case against her was built on testimony that did not hold up under cross-examination. Seven years of one woman's life cannot be returned to her.
An intact criminal-procedure regime for prosecutions against named political opponents is the institutional check the constitution embeds at multiple levels (due process, presumption of innocence, right to confrontation of witnesses). When the prosecution case relies on coordinated testimony from convicts later recanted under threat of further state action, the institutional consequence is not just the wrongful detention; it is the demonstrated playbook for jailing political opponents using mechanisms that look procedurally normal from a distance. Carnegie named this directly in 2019: the "weaponized" legal system, including the imprisonment of Senators De Lima and Trillanes "on government-initiated charges."[89] The 2021-2023 acquittals are the legal receipt; the 6-year-9-month detention is the operational point.
What the next president inherits: a documented playbook for jailing political opponents using coordinated witness testimony, and the public record of what it took (recantations, multiple acquittals, nearly seven years of one woman's life) to undo it. The 2028 due-diligence question: which candidate has committed to the prosecutorial reforms (protected-witness program safeguards, recantation-handling protocols, accountability for prosecutors found to have produced retracted testimony) that make this playbook unrepeatable?
Part VIII · Political Prisoners · 30 · Capture · Trillanes and the amnesty revocation

The first time a Philippine president tried to revoke an amnesty by proclamation. The Supreme Court eventually said: no.

  • 2010: Trillanes granted amnesty via Proclamation No. 75 by then-President Benigno Aquino III; coup d'etat case dismissed 2011.
  • August 31, 2018: Duterte issued Proclamation No. 572, declaring Trillanes's amnesty void ab initio.[136] The Proclamation ordered DOJ, AFP, and PNP to revive prosecutions and "employ all lawful means to apprehend" him.
  • The trial-court split. Makati RTC Branch 148 (Judge Andres Soriano) denied DOJ's bid to revive coup d'etat charges; Branch 150 (Judge Elmo Alameda) reinstated the rebellion case — two judges, one law, different outcomes.[93]
  • The Court of Appeals correction: Branch 150 had committed "grave abuse of discretion amounting to excess of jurisdiction."[137]
  • April 3, 2024 — Supreme Court En Banc: Proclamation 572 ruled unconstitutional. The Court held that a President cannot revoke amnesty without concurrence from Congress — the same constitutional principle that originally required congressional concurrence for the amnesty's grant.[138]
An intact amnesty institution is the constitutional mechanism by which the state, in defined circumstances and with congressional consent, ends prosecutions in service of reconciliation or political-system normalization. The institutional architecture intentionally separates the granting power (President + Congress jointly) from the revoking question (also requiring joint action) precisely so that a successor executive cannot unilaterally reopen cases the prior reconciliation closed. Proclamation 572 attempted to bypass that separation. The Supreme Court's eventual unanimous En Banc rejection of the proclamation was the constitutional system catching up to the unilateral attempt — but the years between 2018 and 2024 during which Trillanes faced revived prosecutions, asset constraints, and personal-safety pressure are the operational cost the constitutional correction cannot reverse. The precedent matters even after defeat: future executives now know exactly which constitutional limit applies and exactly how long it takes the Court to invoke it.
What the next president inherits: the constitutional precedent that amnesty revocation requires congressional concurrence — a check the courts had to manufacture in response to the attempt to bypass it. The 2028 due-diligence question (Watchlist #14): is the candidate's documented position consistent with the SC's April 2024 ruling, or does the candidate seek to challenge it?
Part IX · Foreign Policy + Defense · 31 · Normalization · The West Philippine Sea

The 2016 UNCLOS ruling Filipinos won — set aside, rhetorically, for six years.

  • July 12, 2016: Permanent Court of Arbitration ruled in favor of the Philippines under UNCLOS; China's nine-dash-line claim found to have no legal basis. The ruling came down 12 days into Duterte's term.
  • Duterte's stance. "Set aside" the ruling for the duration of his term in favor of bilateral engagement with China; characterized the ruling in various public speeches as a document whose enforcement he would not actively pursue.
  • The operational cost. Chinese maritime militia + coast guard presence in the WPS became routinized; Filipino fishermen displaced from traditional fishing grounds; Reed Bank, Scarborough Shoal, and Ayungin Shoal became sites of regular confrontation by 2022.
  • 2022 onward: the Marcos administration partially reactivated the UNCLOS ruling rhetorically; assertion missions and joint patrols with allied navies became routine; the WPS posture pivoted from "set aside" toward active sovereignty defense.
An intact foreign-policy assertion of the 2016 UNCLOS ruling is the Philippines's strongest legal instrument against Chinese encroachment in the West Philippine Sea — an instrument the Filipino state spent years and resources securing. Setting the ruling aside, even rhetorically, signalled to Beijing that the Philippine executive would not enforce the ruling and signalled to Filipino fishermen and coastal communities that their access to traditional fishing grounds would not be defended. The institutional consequence reaches beyond foreign policy: the precedent that an incoming president can effectively annul a Philippine legal victory by simply choosing not to invoke it weakens every future Filipino legal claim in international fora. Foreign-policy assertion is not optional after a favorable ruling; it is what converts the ruling into operational protection.
What the next president inherits: six years of accumulated Chinese maritime presence in the WPS, dispossessed Filipino fishing communities, and the precedent that a favorable UNCLOS ruling can be left unenforced by executive choice. The 2028 due-diligence question (Watchlist #9): what is the candidate's stated position on the 2016 UNCLOS ruling's active enforcement?
Part IX · Foreign Policy + Defense · 32 · Capture · AFP modernization pivot

Sixteen Russian military helicopters ordered. Then cancelled. ₱12.7 billion in defense procurement re-routed.

  • November 2021: the Philippines agreed to pay ₱12.7 billion (~$244 million) for 16 Mi-17 helicopters from Russia, sought to modernize AFP hardware.[139] A down payment of ~₱2B was made in January 2022.
  • Duterte's posture on Putin. Publicly called Putin his "idol"; sought Chinese and Russian arms suppliers during his term while criticizing US security cooperation.
  • August 10, 2022: the Marcos administration cancelled the deal over US CAATSA sanctions exposure following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Defense Secretary Delfin Lorenzana: "We could face sanctions."[140]
  • October 2022: the US provided $100 million in foreign military funding as partial compensation; the Philippines pivoted to US/allied platforms.[141]
  • The bureaucratic cost. A ₱12.7B contract negotiated, advanced, and cancelled within ~9 months represents substantial DND/AFP staff time, opportunity cost on platforms not procured, and a re-procurement cycle that delayed AFP modernization timelines.
An intact AFP modernization posture is calibrated to the country's actual security threats (primarily WPS-related external pressure) and to the country's existing alliance commitments (the Mutual Defense Treaty with the US, ASEAN security frameworks). Pivoting procurement toward suppliers with whom the country has no defense-treaty relationship — and whose principal at the time was the subject of internationally-sanctioned military action — created predictable sanctions exposure for the Philippines's own military hardware pipeline. The Mi-17 contract is the institutional artifact of a procurement decision that ran ahead of foreign-policy and sanctions-compliance analysis. The Marcos cancellation was the correction; the down payment, contract, and ~9 months of bureaucratic effort are the cost the correction cannot fully reverse.
What the next president inherits: an AFP modernization pipeline whose 2021-2022 detour through Russian suppliers cost time, money, and alliance-trust. The 2028 due-diligence question: what is the candidate's stated framework for evaluating defense suppliers against sanctions-compliance and alliance-treaty criteria?

Part X · Normalization · 34 · Capture + Normalization · POGOs

An executive order welcomed the industry; the industry brought kidnapping, trafficking, and 60+ killings.

  • 2017: Duterte issued Executive Order 13 institutionalizing licensing of Philippine Offshore Gaming Operators (POGOs).[144]
  • Crime profile through 2022: human trafficking, kidnapping, robbery-extortion, money laundering, online scams, debt bondage, torture of foreign workers, forced labor, prostitution — per Inquirer, PCIJ, GMA, and Senate investigations across the period.[144]
  • Body count. The Presidential Anti-Organized Crime Commission (PAOCC) reported 60-63 POGO-related killings between 2017-2023.[145]
  • National police data: "POGO-related kidnappings" rose 25% in January-September 2020.[144]
  • March 2024 raid (under Marcos): 868 POGO workers rescued from one Pampanga complex — 371 Filipinos, 427 Chinese, 57 Vietnamese, 8 Malaysians, 3 Taiwanese, 2 Indonesians, 2 Rwandans.[146]
  • July 22, 2024: Marcos announced the immediate POGO ban during his State of the Nation Address.
  • November 5, 2024: Marcos formally implemented the ban via Executive Order 74.[146]
An intact gaming-licensing framework calibrates the costs of attracting gambling-industry capital against the externalities the industry imposes (organized-crime infiltration, human trafficking, money laundering). EO 13's 2017 framing tilted the balance heavily toward attracting POGO capital with limited corresponding investment in the immigration enforcement, anti-trafficking infrastructure, and crime-investigation capacity required to handle the externalities at scale. The result was an industry whose operational footprint required regular Filipino police rescues of trafficked foreign workers from facilities the Filipino state had licensed. Marcos's 2024 ban is the corrective; the seven-year POGO-era is the institutional damage that the ban did not undo (relationships, casino-related corruption, foreign-criminal networks now operating in country).
What the next president inherits: the residual networks of POGO-era organized-crime operations and the structural lesson that gambling-industry licensing without proportionate enforcement infrastructure creates predictable trafficking outcomes. The 2028 due-diligence question (Watchlist #13): will the candidate keep POGOs banned, and what is the candidate's commitment on POGO-era organized-crime prosecutions?
Part XI · The Confessions · 35 · Normalization · DDS confessions

Two former insiders. Two Senate confessions. One recanted his denial.

  • September 2016: Edgar Matobato testified to the Senate (then-Sen. Leila de Lima presiding) that he had been a member of the Davao Death Squad and that then-Mayor Rodrigo Duterte had commanded the group.[147]
  • October 2016: Arturo Lascañas initially denied DDS membership, refuting Matobato's testimony.[148]
  • February 20, 2017: Lascañas reversed his denial and testified before the Senate that he had been a DDS member; the group had operated in Davao from the mid-1990s to as recently as 2013. He stated his earlier denial had been ordered by Duterte's longtime aide SPO4 Sonny Buenaventura, who told him his family would be at risk if he testified truthfully.[148]
  • What Lascañas testified: he killed almost 200 people, was paid by Duterte; DDS killed not only criminals but Duterte's political and media enemies.[148]
  • The Philippine government characterization at the time: Lascañas's testimony was "fabrication." Philstar's detailed analysis at the time concluded the two testimonies "generally match."[149]
  • The ICC predicate. The DDS-era Davao killings (Nov 2011 onward, while Duterte was Mayor and Vice Mayor) sit within the ICC's jurisdictional window for the ongoing investigation (cross-reference Slide 13).
  • Methodology note: The brief presents the Matobato and Lascañas Senate testimonies as a matter of public record. It does not adjudicate which testimony is true. It observes that the testimonies are now part of the international-prosecution record being weighed by the ICC.
An intact Senate investigative authority is the parliamentary instrument by which alleged state-perpetrated crimes that domestic prosecutors cannot or will not pursue can nonetheless be entered into the public record under oath. The Matobato (September 2016) and Lascañas (February 2017) Senate testimonies were the first sworn insider accounts of the Davao Death Squad before the international community's institutional memory. The Senate hearings did not produce convictions — they produced sworn testimony. That testimony became the predicate for the eventual ICC investigation (Slide 13), the De Lima case (Slide 29 — she presided over the early hearings before her own detention), and the international accountability architecture that the ICC March 2025 arrest of Duterte operationalized.
What the next president inherits: a documentary record of alleged state-perpetrated killings entered into the Senate's institutional memory and the international-prosecution chain. The 2028 due-diligence question: does the candidate publicly commit to cooperating with the ICC investigation that the Senate testimonies helped predicate?
