What actually makes a rapist, a killer, a thief — and what doesn't.

Thirteen slides on the composite lie that justified Duterte's drug war — the rhetoric that drug addicts are rapists, killers, and thieves who deserve to die. The folly of believing it. Folly #1 · the rape justification

Drug user ≠ rapist.
Wrong in both directions — and the war was built on it.

→ Forward direction
"If they take drugs, they will rape."
Most drug users never commit sexual violence.
Hundreds of millions use drugs globally; the rape-perpetration subset is tiny. The personality, attitudes, and past experiences of perpetrators who drink during assault are the same as those who don't drink (Abbey et al., PMC peer-reviewed review). Substance use can disinhibit; it does not create a rapist disposition.
← Reverse direction
"Rapists are mostly drug-dependent addicts."
Most rapists are not drug-dependent.
Rape is driven by anger, power, and control — not sexual desire (Groth typology, OJP/DOJ-cited). Most rapes are planned, not impulsive. Roughly ~70 % are committed by someone the victim already knows — acquaintance, partner, ex-partner (RAINN, US data).

Drugs are an easy story. The actual story is older, more uncomfortable, and lives in how a person was shaped before any substance entered the picture.

To anyone reading this who has survived sexual assault — whenever it happened, whoever did it, and whether the perpetrator used substances or not: your experience is real, and what was done to you was never your fault. The research patterns above describe populations of perpetrators, not your story. Statistics cannot measure your pain, and they are not meant to. They are here for one reason only: to show that the cartoon used to justify EJKs does not match the science — and that the killings did not protect survivors like you. The unfinished work of real safety, real justice, and real accountability for the person who harmed you is still owed to you.
SOURCES · OJP/DOJ Groth typology (PubMed 910975); RAINN perpetrator-victim data; PMC 3177166 Abbey et al., Alcohol's Role in Sexual Violence Perpetration
Folly #2 · the "shoot them dead" justification

Drug user ≠ killer.
Only 3.9 % of US homicides are narcotics-related — yet we shot tens of thousands here.

→ Forward direction
"Addicts become violent — they will kill you."
Most drug users are non-violent.
The drug-crime meta-analysis (odds of any offending 2.8–3.8× for users vs. non-users) covers all crime categories — the violent-crime subset is much smaller. WHO load-bearing risk factors for interpersonal violence: harmful masculinity norms, witnessing family violence, weapon availability. Not pharmacology.
← Reverse direction
"Murderers are mostly drug-crazed addicts."
Most homicide perpetrators are not drug-dependent.
Per BJS / FBI UCR (2007): only 3.9 % of US homicides with known circumstances were narcotics-related. Per BJS 2004 Survey of Inmates: violent offenders were only 10 % likely to have committed the crime for drug money (vs. 30 % property, 26 % drug). Most "drug-related" violence is systemic — drug-market turf disputes (Goldstein 1985, OJP) — not the cartoon addict who snapped.

Homicide is overwhelmingly driven by interpersonal disputes, intimate-partner violence, and gang/economic conflict — not by what someone smoked yesterday.

To anyone grieving someone lost to homicide — drug-related, vigilante, EJK, domestic, accident-disguised-as-violence, or any cause at all, whether years ago or recent: this deck does not minimize your loss. Statistics are how we expose policy failures; they are not how we measure your grief. Your loved one was not a number. The numbers exist here to show that the policy did not honor them either — it killed in their name without protecting anyone, and it never delivered the justice their case deserved.
SOURCES · BJS Drug-Related Crime & Drugs & Crime Facts; OJP Goldstein 1985 tripartite framework; WHO Violence Against Women fact sheet
Folly #3 · the "thief" justification

Drug user ≠ thief — and where the link is real, it points to treatment, not bullets.
The folly was answering theft with murder.

→ Forward direction
"Addicts will steal from you."
Most drug users do not commit property crime.
The link concentrates in severe heroin/crack dependence under poverty (Goldstein's "economic-compulsive" subset). Casual users, recreational users, mild-SUD cases do not systematically steal. The drug is rarely the variable that flips a non-thief into a thief — resourcelessness is.
← Reverse direction
"Thieves are mostly drug-addicted."
More mixed — but still wrong as a sweeping claim.
~85 % of burglary inmates report substance dependence or abuse (the highest-link offense category, per BJS). But only ~25 % of property offenders said they committed the crime for drug money. And the OJP Professional Thieves and Drugs study found criminal activity began before drug use — addiction was incidental to their criminal lifestyle.

The honest answer: the substance ↔ theft link is the strongest of the three — but it is conditional, narrow, and the implied solution is treatment + economic safety nets. Not bullets. Never bullets.

To anyone, anywhere, who has been hurt by theft, addiction-adjacent crime, or a loved one's substance struggle — whether during the drug war or long before it: your fear was, and is, real. The case here is not that the harm you experienced was imaginary — it is that bullets in alleys did not protect you, and the kingpins were never jailed. The data shows it. You deserved real safety: rehab access, livelihood, community policing, due process for the few who do harm, and accountability all the way up the chain. Not a war that killed your neighbors and left the actual traffickers untouched.
SOURCES · BJS Drugs & Crime Facts; OJP Professional Thieves and Drugs; Goldstein 1985 "economic-compulsive" framework
The folly of ignoring the actual causes

It was never drugs. It was childhood, attachment, and how violence was modeled.
We killed a symptom and called it a cure.

76 %

78 %

68 %
physical abuse in childhood
70 %

WHO

None of these factors is "took drugs." If we wanted to actually reduce sexual violence, the evidence-based interventions would be early-childhood support, ending corporal punishment, addressing masculinity norms, and family-violence prevention. Killing the symptom in an alley does nothing for the cause.

The cartoon that fuelled the folly

When the pulpit said "drug addict," what did you picture?
The image in your head was always class-coded — and that image did the killing before any bullet did.

The cartoon (what we pictured)
A shabu-crazed predator from the slum.
Male. Poor. Tattooed. Unpredictable. Dangerous to your daughter. Already half a criminal. Beyond help. Beyond redemption. A "hopeless case". A justified casualty.
The actual population
Students. Workers. Parents. Recovering people. Mild-SUD cases.
A college student who experimented once. A nurse with prescription dependence. A jeepney driver self-medicating chronic pain. A teenager who tried weed. A neighbor in mild SUD. Most are not violent, not impulsive, not predatory. Most were never going to harm anyone.

The cartoon was always already half a verdict. The image did the killing before any bullet did. And the cartoon was never the businessman with a coke habit or the mayor's son with a pill problem. The cartoon, by design, lived in the slum.

SOURCES · NIDA population-level use vs. addiction prevalence; Philippine DDB drug-survey context
The targeting folly · who actually died

"If you are poor, you are killed."
— Amnesty International report title, 2017. The war on drugs was a war on the poor.

~30,000
drug-war deaths concentrated in urban-poor barangays — Caloocan, Tondo, Payatas, Pasig informal settlements. Almost none in gated subdivisions.
"Oplan Tokhang had to target the poor because they have no capacity to sue." — Insider witness testimony, ICC pre-trial · via Rappler

Meanwhile — according to AMLC findings now confirmed in House Justice Committee hearings — alleged drug lord Samuel "Sammy" Uy paid the Duterte family ₱181.6 million in checks between 2011 and 2013. He was never raided. Never on a tokhang list. Wealthy addicts went to private rehab. Politicians' sons caught with drugs faced lawyers, plea deals, and reputation management — not bullets in alleys.

It wasn't a war on drugs. It was a war on people who couldn't fight back.

And the deepest folly: the people the public most wanted off the streets — the actual traffickers, kingpins, corrupt cops, and rapists who had power — were not jailed. Tens of thousands of suspects were killed without trial; almost zero kingpins were convicted. The countless shortcuts — barangay-list hearsay, anonymous drop-box tips, planted guns, no lawyer, no due process — bypassed every step that could have produced a real conviction. Real victims of crime got no justice. Real perpetrators with money or rank walked away. The shortcuts were the policy. The shortcuts were the failure.

Allegations cited above are drawn from public proceedings — ICC charges and warrants, Senate Blue Ribbon and House Justice Committee hearings, Ombudsman filings, COA reports, AMLC disclosures — and from verified press reporting (Amnesty, HRW, Rappler, Inquirer, GMA News, Mindanews). Where cases remain unadjudicated, “alleged” is implied. All persons named are presumed innocent until convicted by a court of competent jurisdiction.

The folly of distance

You were never going to be on the list.
And that is why the killings did not break your sleep.

The Christians who supported — or quietly tolerated — the drug war were overwhelmingly not the people being killed. The dead came from informal settlements: Caloocan alleys, Tondo backlots, Payatas slopes. The pulpit congregations came from gated subdivisions, condominiums, professional-class suburbs. Two Philippines. One paid for the other's policy.

It was easy to support harsher judgment for the poor because the harsh judgment was always happening to other people. The pulpit said "drug addict" and the comfortable Christian heard "those people in the slum" — not "my cousin, my uncle, my neighbor." The cartoon worked precisely because the audience was insulated from the cost.

If 30,000 had been killed in Forbes Park, BGC, or Ayala Heights, the drug war would have been stopped in a week.

"If you really keep the royal law found in Scripture, 'Love your neighbor as yourself,' you are doing right. But if you show favoritism, you sin and are convicted by the law as lawbreakers. … You have dishonored the poor. Is it not the rich who are exploiting you?"

James 2:8–9, 6 (NIV)

Christian elitism does not look like cruelty. It looks like distance. Like quiet relief that the trouble is in someone else's barangay. Like a sermon that names "the drug problem" without ever naming a person who might still be alive. That distance is the folly. That distance was the political condition for the killings.

SOURCES · Amnesty International ASA 35/5517/2017; HRW; Rappler mapping of EJK incidents by barangay socio-economic status; James 2:1–9 (NIV); cf. Proverbs 14:31, Amos 5:11–12, Luke 4:18
The procedural folly · how the lists were made

How a name landed on a tokhang list.
No DSM-5. No clinician. No lawyer. No right of appeal. And in thousands of cases — no trial.

01
Barangay captain submits a list of suspected "drug personalities" in their area
02
BADAC (Barangay Anti-Drug Abuse Council) curates — often relying on community gossip, neighborhood grievances, political enemies
03
Anonymous drop boxes in barangay halls — residents could drop names with no verification
04
Police "validation" — intelligence units check the list. No medical assessment. No DSM-5 criteria. No clinical professional involved.
05
Validation chain: chief of police → provincial director → regional director → Directorate for Intelligence
06
"Knock and plead" visit — or in thousands of cases, the bullet

By design, this methodology guaranteed the poor would be over-represented and the wealthy invisible. A barangay captain in Forbes Park doesn't make a list of his neighbors. A maid in Tondo gets her brother named because of a quarrel over a fence.

The resolution · naming the folly, refusing the impunity

We can share the lament on both sides.
We cannot share the impunity. We cannot bless the folly.

What we share
Grief for every innocent life.

The family abused by addiction is innocent. The child harmed by a strung-out neighbor is innocent. The community traumatized by a meth house is innocent. Their grief is real. Their cry for safety is righteous.

We are not minimizing their pain. Solving the drug problem and refusing extrajudicial killings are not opposites. Demanding justice for EJK victims does not mean abandoning communities to addiction. Both can be true. Both must be.

What we refuse
Forbearance for state-sanctioned murder.

You cannot extend "forbearance" to theft and killing dressed up as policy. That is not reconciliation — that is collaboration. The Bible asks forbearance between equals in disagreement; it never asks it of the powerful crushing the powerless.

We cannot wait for this violence to reach our own families before we wake up. Impunity is the actual enemy here — the power to kill, steal, and walk away unaccountable. That is what we are fighting, not addicts in slums.
