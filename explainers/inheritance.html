02 · Abstract, addressed to you

This letter is to you.

A Filipino voter, between 18 and 30, who will help decide the 2028 election. You are inheriting a country shaped by choices made before you could vote. This brief is one attempt to lay out, with sources, what the public record so far shows a Sara Duterte presidency would mean for the next four years of your life.

Sara Duterte is the declared frontrunner for the 2028 Philippine presidential election[1]. She is currently on trial in the Senate, sitting as an impeachment court, after becoming the first Philippine official ever impeached twice (May 11, 2026)[2]. Four consolidated articles cite ₱612.5 million in misused confidential funds, unexplained wealth, and threats against the President[3].

Methodology, in one paragraph.

Every factual claim carries a numbered citation linked to a live source on the References slide. Sources are first-tier Philippine and international news (Rappler, Inquirer, Al Jazeera, Bloomberg, GMA, Philstar, BusinessWorld, MindaNews, Tribune, Manila Bulletin, ANC, CMFR, VERA Files, Time, Reuters, RSF, CPJ); Philippine institutions of audit and law (COA, AMLC, PSA, BSP, DoLE, the OVP's own published reports); international accountability and academic bodies (HRW, Amnesty, Civicus, OCCRP, IFJ, UNICEF, ILO, ADB, The Lancet); and polling (Pulse Asia, OCTA, SWS, WR Numero, ICHRP, Germanwatch). Wikipedia is used for chronology only, never as the primary source for a claim.

Two honesty guards that recur in this brief.

  • On AMLC findings: AMLC reports a transaction's existence, not its legal interpretation. Only a court rules on guilt. We cite AMLC findings as evidence of what is on record, not as a verdict.
  • On her conduct and her attention: this brief makes no diagnostic claim about Sara Duterte's mental state and no claim about her inner motives. It cites authenticated public conduct, named credentialed observers, and observable scheduling patterns. You evaluate fitness for office.

Scope: focuses on Sara Duterte's individual record and stated plans. Not a comparative evaluation of other 2028 candidates.

07 · Methodology · How AMLC data works

Why this evidence is hard to fake.

A first move of any defense is to call the data political. Here is the legal architecture that makes AMLC findings the most evidentiary class of finding in this brief.

Panel 1 · Where the data comes from
  • AMLC is the Philippines' financial intelligence unit, created under Republic Act 9160 (Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2001) and amended by RA 9194, 10167, 10365, 10927, and 11521.[39]
  • Banks, casinos, real-estate brokers, and other "covered persons" are legally required to submit two kinds of reports:
    • Covered Transaction Reports (CTRs) — any single-day cash or transfer transaction over ₱500,000.[39]
    • Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs) — any transaction, regardless of amount, with no clear legal purpose or otherwise unusual.[39]
  • Reports must be filed within 5 working days.[39]
Panel 2 · What happens to banks that get it wrong
  • Failure to keep records: 6 months–1 year imprisonment + ₱100K–₱500K fine for responsible bank officers.[40]
  • Willful blindness / intentional failure to report: criminal charges + imprisonment.[40]
  • Money laundering itself: 7–14 years + fines ≥ ₱3M.[40]
  • False or malicious reporting: 6 months–4 years + ₱100K–₱500K fine.[40]
  • Banks have bilateral skin in the game — punished for under-reporting and for malicious false reporting.
Panel 3 · What protects banks reporting in good faith
  • Safe harbor: "no administrative, criminal or civil proceedings shall lie against any person for having made a covered or suspicious transaction report in good faith."[40]
  • This is by design — it removes the disincentive to report. A wealthy account can generate hundreds of CTRs without any individual report being accusatory.
  • Translation: a bank that filed reports on Sara Duterte's accounts faced legal exposure if it withheld the data, and legal protection for submitting it.
Panel 4 · If AMLC officials modify the data
  • AMLC officials are public officers; AMLA imposes administrative sanctions plus criminal liability for breach of confidentiality (3–8 years + ₱500K–₱1M, §9(c)).[40]
  • Public officials convicted face perpetual or temporary absolute disqualification from office in addition to the penalties.[40]
  • AMLC is governed by an inter-agency council (BSP, SEC, IC) — no single political principal controls it.[39]
  • Banks retain their own copies under the 5-year records-keeping rule. Any discrepancy is auditable by BSP, the Senate impeachment court, or the courts.[39]
  • For AMLC's compilation to be fake, multiple independent banks would have had to simultaneously fabricate consistent reports — each facing criminal exposure.
The AMLC system is the reason a sitting Vice President's bank movements can be cited under oath without the prosecution subpoenaing her cooperation. The data does not depend on her consent, on a single journalist's reporting, or on a single political party. It is the operational by-product of a regulated reporting regime with bilateral criminal exposure on both reporters and recipients.

Frenchie Mae Cumpio — the precedent, not a direct charge.

Filipino journalist born January 23, 1999; executive director of the Eastern Vista news site, radio news anchor in Tacloban. Arrested February 2020 under the Rodrigo Duterte administration; convicted January 21, 2026 by the Tacloban Regional Trial Court of financing terrorism; sentenced to 12 years 5 months to 18 years 8 months. The first journalist in the Philippines convicted of financing terrorism, the country's longest-imprisoned journalist in recent years. Sixteen foreign embassies issued statements troubled by the verdict.[37]

Honest attribution: Sara Duterte has no documented direct role in Cumpio's arrest or prosecution. Cumpio is cited here as the precedent for what the Anti-Terrorism Act of 2020 (RA 11479) — passed during her father's administration — enables, and which a Sara Duterte presidency would inherit and preserve.

The Vice President's own words about silencing, in her own video.

I gave my orders."[31] The NBI later authenticated the video as unedited and found "reasonable certainty of conviction" for inciting to sedition and grave threats.[38] Three journalists were subpoenaed by NBI Cybercrime Division after that press conference.[41]

Speaking out gets more legally expensive. Community organizing gets more dangerous. Journalism that covers power gets reframed as a crime — and the precedent for that reframing already has a name and a sentence.

  • Cross-link to the sibling reckonings on this site (not-made-to-live-in-the-lie.html, duterte-drug-war-follies.html) for the class pattern and the central composite lie.

The sovereign debt your generation will pay.

  • When Rodrigo Duterte took office (June 2016), national debt stood at ₱5.948 trillion. When he stepped down (June 30, 2022), it stood at ₱12.791 trillion.[50]
  • The administration added ₱6.843 trillion in new borrowings in six years — exceeding the combined borrowings of all previous Philippine administrations on the modern record.[50][51]
  • Debt-to-GDP rose to 63.5% at end of 2021 — a 17-year high.[50]
  • Drivers per official statements and reporting: pandemic response, Build, Build, Build, and procurement opacity (the Pharmally scandal — nearly US$175M in overpriced COVID contracts to a firm capitalized at ~US$12,000 — is the most-cited single illustration).[52]

Sovereign debt does not vanish at the end of a president's term. It is repaid out of future tax revenues — the income tax, VAT, and excise you will pay across the working life that is now beginning for Gen Z. A Sara Duterte presidency that does not break with the borrowing patterns and procurement opacity of the prior administration means a longer repayment shadow over your career.

The institutions hollowed out before her — a partial inventory.

A Sara Duterte presidency is not a choice about Sara Duterte alone. It is a choice about whether to reinstall the impunity machine that protected her father — into a country whose checking institutions are now meaningfully less capable of restraining it than they were when her father took office.
